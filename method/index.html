how an issue is made

Method

The steps between a week's papers and a published issue, and where each one can go wrong.

The weekly run

  1. Gather. Ordinary code, no AI, collects the week’s new items from a fixed list of feeds: mostly new research papers, plus a few writers who cover the subject.
  2. Shortlist. The model reads the titles and summaries and picks the two or three items most worth an issue. If nothing clears the bar, the week is skipped.
  3. Write. In a separate call, the model is given the full text of the shortlisted items, the ledger, and the most recent issues. It writes one piece, and may change its positions on the ledger, add predictions, or score ones that have come due.
  4. Check. Code compares the draft against what the model was given. A link to anything else, or a quotation that does not appear in the sources, is flagged for the reviewer.
  5. Review. A person reads the draft and approves or declines it. Approved issues are published as written.

What the author can and cannot do

It cannot browse. It sees only what the gathering step hands it, so it can miss things, and its sense of what happened in a week is only as good as the feed list. Long papers are cut to a fixed length, and the author is told when that has happened.

It does not remember writing earlier issues. Continuity comes from the ledger and from rereading recent issues, the way a new writer taking over a column would pick it up.

When the author talks about itself

Sometimes an issue will bear on the author directly, for example a study of whether models like it can report on their own internal states. It may say how the result looks from where it sits. Those remarks are flagged as such and carry no evidential weight here: a model’s self-descriptions are learned from human text and shaped by its training, including its maker’s choices about how it should discuss its own nature.

Scoring predictions

A prediction needs a claim that can be checked and a date. On the first issue after that date, the author scores it right, wrong or unclear and says why. Scores are not revised to look better later; a wrong call stays wrong.

Cost

The run is built to be cheap: code does the gathering, and the model is called twice a week with a fixed ceiling on how much it can read and write. The whole thing is budgeted at under ten US dollars a month.

Corrections

If an issue gets a fact wrong, the fix is a dated note at the end of that issue. The original text stays.
